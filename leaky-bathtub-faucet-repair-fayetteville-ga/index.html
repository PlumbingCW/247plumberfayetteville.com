How to Fix a Leaky Bathtub Faucet in Fayetteville, GA

Written by the Fayetteville 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 23, 2026.

To fix a leaky bathtub faucet in Fayetteville, GA, first work out where the water is coming from. A drip from the spout usually means a worn diverter or a failing valve inside the wall. A drip from the handle points to a worn cartridge or stem. Shut off the water, swap the worn part, and the leak normally stops.

The tricky bit is telling the three apart. This guide walks through the clues, the shutoff question, a general-level repair sequence, and the point where a plumber is the better call.

Start by watching the drip

Do not pull anything apart yet. Turn the handle off firmly, dry the tub and the wall plate, and watch for a few minutes. Where the first bead of water forms tells you most of what you need to know. A drip that keeps going while the handle is fully off means water is leaking past a part that should be sealing.

Also notice when it happens. A drip that only appears right after a shower is often water draining out of the pipe behind the wall. A steady drip all day points to a worn seal. If only the hot side drips, the problem is usually the hot-side stem or cartridge.

Spout, valve, or cartridge: reading the clues

Most tub faucets leak from one of three places. The table matches what you see to the part that is usually failing.

Where a tub faucet leak comes from
What you seeLikely partTypical fix
Water drips from the spout when offCartridge, stem, or valve seatReplace the cartridge or the washer and seat
Water sprays from the spout in a weak stream while the shower runsDiverter that no longer sealsReplace the diverter or the spout
Water leaks around the handleWorn O-ring or loose packingReplace the O-ring or tighten the packing nut
Water seeps where the spout meets the wallFailed seal or loose spoutReseal or tighten the spout, or swap it
Hot water only dripsWorn hot-side stem or cartridgeReplace that side only
Wet drywall or ceiling belowLeak in the wall or at a valve bodyOpen access and inspect the pipe

If you have two handles, you are likely dealing with compression stems and rubber washers. A single handle usually means a cartridge or a ball-style valve. Both kinds wear out, and replacement parts are made for the specific brand, so take the old part with you when shopping.

Finding the shutoff before you touch the handle

Some homes have small stop valves behind an access panel on the other side of the tub wall, in a closet, or in a basement ceiling. Many do not. If there are no local stops, shut off the main water valve for the house and open a faucet elsewhere to drain pressure.

Know where the main shutoff is before you need it, and try turning it once in a calm moment. A valve that has not moved in years can stick or break. If it will not close, stop and call a plumber, because forcing it is how a small drip turns into a flood.

Plug the drain first

Small screws and clips drop straight into the tub drain. Stuff a rag in the drain opening and lay a towel in the tub before you remove a handle or trim.

A general-level repair sequence

The exact steps vary by brand, and the instructions that come with the replacement part are the best guide. The outline below covers the usual order for a cartridge or stem leak.

  1. 1

    Shut off the water

    Close the stop valves or the main, then open the faucet to confirm the flow has stopped.

  2. 2

    Remove the handle and trim

    Look for a cap or set screw. Pry the cap gently, loosen the screw, and slide the handle off.

  3. 3

    Pull the cartridge or stem

    Unscrew the retaining nut or clip. Note how the old part sits so the new one goes back the same way.

  4. 4

    Inspect the seat and O-rings

    Look for pitting, mineral crust, or torn rubber. Clean the seat gently and replace worn O-rings.

  5. 5

    Install, reassemble, and test

    Seat the new part, tighten the nut by hand and a little more, turn the water back on slowly, and check for drips.

Do not over-tighten anything, and do not force a part that resists. A seized cartridge sometimes needs a puller made for that brand. That is a common stopping point for homeowners.

Jobs that belong to a plumber

  • No shutoff that works: the main or local stop will not close fully.
  • Stuck cartridge or corroded stem: parts that snap or round off are hard to recover.
  • Leak inside the wall: stains on the ceiling below or a damp wall near the tub.
  • Valve body replaced: when the valve behind the tile has failed, it may mean opening the wall.
  • Repeated leaks after a repair: the seat or valve body is often the real problem.

If you see signs of water inside a wall, the right first step is finding the source, which is the work behind leak detection.

For the faucet itself, dripping handles, cartridges, and shower valves are covered on the faucet repair page. A dripping tub faucet is not usually an emergency, but a leak behind the wall can be, so shut the water off if you see it spreading.
