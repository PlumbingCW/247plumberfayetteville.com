Gas Tankless Water Heaters for Fayetteville, GA Homes

Written by the Fayetteville 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 23, 2026.

Yes, a gas tankless water heater can work in a Fayetteville, GA home, but the house has to be ready for it. The unit needs a properly sized gas line, a vent path that suits its design, and a water flow rate that matches how many fixtures run at once. Those three checks decide whether the retrofit is simple or turns into a bigger project.

This post covers what to measure before you buy, what a retrofit usually involves, and the maintenance that keeps a tankless unit running well.

Start with the gas line, not the heater

A tankless heater burns a lot of gas for a short time. A standard tank has a modest burner that runs longer, so the pipe that served it may be too small for a tankless unit. The plumber adds up the gas demand of every appliance on that line, then compares it to what the pipe, the meter, and the regulator can deliver.

If the numbers fall short, the fix is usually a larger gas branch from the meter or a nearer tee. That is real work, and it should appear as its own line on the quote. Gas line work is a licensed job, and permit rules vary, so ask the Fayetteville building department before any change.

Gas smell comes first

If you smell gas near any appliance, leave the building right away and call 911 or your gas utility from outside. Do not flip switches or look for the source. Call a plumber only after the utility says it is safe.

Venting: what changes from a tank heater

Many tankless units run hotter and push exhaust out through special sealed vent pipe, often plastic rated by the maker, rather than the metal flue a tank uses. Condensing models pull cooler exhaust and also produce acidic condensate that must drain somewhere. Non-condensing models vent hotter and need a different material.

The vent must be the material, diameter, and length the manufacturer specifies, and it must terminate where the code and the maker allow. Intake air matters too. Some units draw combustion air from the room, and others pipe it from outside. A garage or closet that worked for a tank may need louvers, or a different route.

Flow rate and temperature rise

Tankless sizing is about gallons per minute, not tank gallons. Add up the fixtures you might run together, such as a shower, a dishwasher, and a laundry cycle, then check the heater's rating at the temperature rise your cold supply requires. Incoming water is colder in winter, so a unit that feels strong in spring may struggle in January.

What a retrofit usually involves

Swapping a tank for a tankless unit is rarely a straight replacement. The old heater comes out, the wall space is rebuilt, and the new unit mounts on the wall with its own connections. Isolation valves go on the water side so the unit can be flushed later.

  • Gas: confirm capacity, add or resize a branch, and pressure test the connections.
  • Vent: run the correct vent material and set the termination.
  • Power: most gas units still need a small electrical supply for the board and fan.
  • Condensate: route it to a suitable drain if the model makes it.
  • Water: install shutoff and service valves, and handle the old tank piping.

A gas-to-gas swap is common. A move from an electric tank to a gas tankless unit is a bigger change because it adds gas and venting that were not there. Our tankless water heater page explains the install and service options in more detail.

Keeping it healthy: maintenance in plain terms

A tankless heater has a small, tight heat exchanger. Mineral scale narrows it over time, which shows up as longer waits, lukewarm output, or error codes. A periodic flush with the right valves and solution clears it. Follow the manufacturer's interval, because it differs by model and by how scale-prone your water is.

Also clean the inlet screen if the model has one, check the vent for damage, and look for drips at the connections. If an error code keeps returning, write it down. The code tells a technician where to start. Our water heater installation team can also advise on valves and layout for a planned upgrade.

Gas tankless or stay with a tank?

A tankless unit can make hot water as long as the burner runs, which suits big households and long showers. It also frees wall or floor space. A tank is simpler, cheaper to maintain, and gentler on an older gas line.

If your house has a tight gas supply, a hard vent route, or modest hot water use, ask the plumber to price both options side by side. That comparison costs a phone call. When you are ready, call (770) 214-4545 and describe your current heater.

Fayetteville homes and water supply

The City of Fayetteville's 2025 water quality report says three deep wells supply about half the city's water, with the Fayette County Water System providing the rest. The county system draws from Lake Kedron, Lake Peachtree, Lake Horton, and Lake McIntosh. Neither report publishes a hardness figure.

That is why a flush interval cannot be guessed from the address. Test your own tap, and ask the utility for its annual water quality report.

In a house where much of the housing stock grew up in the 1990s and 2000s, the original gas line may also have been sized for a tank, so the line check is worth doing early. The same steps apply in Peachtree City, which has its own sewer authority.
