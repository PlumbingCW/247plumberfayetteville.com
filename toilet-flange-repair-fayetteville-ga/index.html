Toilet Flange Installation and Repair in Fayetteville, GA

Written by the Fayetteville 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 23, 2026.

A toilet flange is the ring that bolts your toilet to the floor and connects it to the drain pipe below.

If it is cracked, rusted, or sitting too low, the toilet rocks, leaks at the base, or lets sewer odor into the bathroom. Toilet flange installation and repair in Fayetteville, GA usually means a repair ring for light damage or a new flange when the old one has failed.

This guide explains how to tell which situation you have, what the floor has to do with it, and where a homeowner should stop and hand the job to a licensed plumber.

What the flange does under the toilet

The flange sits on top of the drain pipe, either resting on the finished floor or set just below it. Two slotted bolts hold the toilet down. A wax ring or a similar gasket fills the gap between the flange and the toilet outlet, so water and gas go down the drain instead of onto the floor.

Flanges are made from PVC, cast iron, brass, or stainless steel. Metal ones can rust. Plastic ones can crack when a toilet is over-tightened or the floor moves. Either way, the seal only works when the toilet sits flat on a solid flange.

Clues that the flange is the problem, not the toilet

Homeowners usually notice the toilet first. A rocking seat, a damp spot that returns after wiping, or a faint sewer smell near the base all point down to the seal. These are the common ways it shows up:

  • Rocking: the bowl shifts when you sit down, even after you snug the closet bolts.
  • Water at the base: moisture appears after a flush, not just after a bath or a mop.
  • Sewer smell: a gas odor that stays near the toilet and gets worse after flushing.
  • Soft or stained floor: vinyl lifts, tile grout darkens, or the floor feels spongy near the bowl.
  • Slow flushing with gurgles: sometimes the flange has sagged and pinched the drain opening.
  • Bolts that spin freely: the flange is cracked or no longer anchored to the floor.

A leak at the base can also come from a loose tank connection or a bad supply line, so a plumber checks those first. If the leak only shows up when the toilet is flushed, the seal under the bowl is the likely source. Other toilet problems are covered on the toilet repair page.

Repair ring, spacer, or full replacement

Not every damaged flange needs to come out. The right fix depends on how much of the ring is broken and what the flange is bolted to. The table below shows how plumbers usually sort the options.

Flange repair options compared
Condition foundTypical fixTrade-off
Slots cracked, pipe and floor soundStainless repair ring screwed over the old flangeFast, but needs solid floor under the ring
Flange sits below the finished floorFlange extender or thicker spacer, new wax ringWorks only if the pipe below is intact
Plastic flange broken off the pipeCut out and replace with a new flangeMore labor, cleaner result
Cast iron flange rusted throughReplace the flange or the short pipe sectionMay open the floor around the drain
Subfloor soft or rottedRepair the floor first, then set a new flangeLongest job, but the only lasting fix

A repair ring is a good answer for a flange that is cracked at the bolt slots. A full replacement is better when the pipe itself has moved or the flange has pulled away. Avoid stacking several wax rings in a gap, because a thick pile of wax can squash out and fail.

Why the floor decides how long the repair lasts

A flange can only hold what it is screwed into. If the plywood around the drain has soaked up water for months, new screws will not bite and the toilet will rock again. This is why a plumber presses on the floor and checks for soft spots before choosing a part.

Remodels cause a different problem. New tile or thicker flooring raises the floor height, and the flange ends up sitting low in a pocket. A low flange leaves a gap that a standard wax ring cannot always seal. Extenders exist for this, but they must be matched to the real gap.

Limits of a quick fix

If the subfloor is rotted, a repair ring is a patch on a patch. The honest answer is to open the floor, repair the wood, and set the flange on solid ground. It is a bigger job, but it lasts.

How a wax ring replacement actually goes

A wax ring swap is a reasonable DIY job when the flange and floor are fine. Stop and call a plumber if you find a broken flange, black rot, or a drain pipe that wobbles.

  1. 1

    Shut off and drain

    Close the supply valve, flush, and sponge out the remaining water in the tank and bowl.

  2. 2

    Lift the toilet

    Disconnect the supply line, remove the nuts from the closet bolts, and lift straight up. The bowl is heavy, so get help.

  3. 3

    Inspect the flange

    Scrape off old wax and look for cracks, rust, and loose screws. Press the floor around it.

  4. 4

    Set the new ring and lower the bowl

    Place the ring on the flange or bowl outlet, guide the bolts through the holes, and press down evenly without twisting.

  5. 5

    Tighten and test

    Snug the nuts a little at a time, alternating sides. Flush several times and watch the base for moisture.

When to bring in a plumber

Call when the flange is cracked, the floor is soft, the drain pipe moves, or the same toilet has leaked twice after a wax ring swap. A plumber can also reset a flange during a bathroom remodel so the height matches the new floor. Work that moves fixtures or adds new drain runs falls under plumbing installation, and the technician can explain what applies.

Pricing depends on the flange material, how much floor repair is involved, whether the pipe below needs work, and whether the toilet itself is being reused.
