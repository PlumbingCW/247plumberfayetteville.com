Most toilet problems come down to a handful of parts: the fill valve, the flapper, the wax ring, the flange and the drain path. Many repairs are finished in a single visit, and the price is clear before work starts.

Reading the symptom: what your toilet is telling you

Homeowners describe toilet trouble in plain terms: it runs on and off by itself, the handle feels loose, the bowl fills slowly, or the base rocks when you sit. The table matches the common ones to a part.

Common toilet symptoms, likely causes and typical fixes

Water on the floor or a bowl that keeps rising

An overflowing bowl or a steady leak at the base is the one toilet problem that should not wait for morning. Our dispatch line answers at night and on weekends, and same-day appointments are available when the schedule allows.

  1. 1

    Stop the water

    Turn the supply valve behind the toilet clockwise. If it will not move, close the main shutoff for the house.

  2. 2

    Lift the tank lid

    If the bowl is rising, push the flapper down into its seat. That stops more water entering the bowl.

  3. 3

    Do not flush again

    Another flush on a blocked line only adds to the spill.

  4. 4

    Soak up what you can

    Towels and a wet vac keep water from reaching subfloor edges and neighboring rooms.

Skip drain chemicals. They sit in the bowl, burn skin, and make the plumber's work less safe.

The parts that fail, and why they fail

The flapper is a rubber seal at the bottom of the tank. It softens, warps, or collects mineral film over time, and then it stops sealing. That is the most common cause of a toilet that runs when nobody has touched it.

The fill valve controls how much water enters the tank after a flush. When its seal wears, you hear a hiss, and the tank may keep topping off.

Below the bowl, a wax ring (or a modern wax-free seal) sits between the toilet and the flange, which is the ring bolted to the floor. If the toilet was ever knocked loose, or the flange rusted or cracked, that seal leaks. Leaks here run into the subfloor, so a soft spot or stained flooring around the base deserves a prompt look.

When the plunger fails: stubborn clogs

A flange-style plunger and steady, patient pressure clear most blockages. A toilet auger reaches farther without scratching the porcelain. If you have tried both and the bowl still drains slowly, the clog may be past the toilet itself.

That is the point to check other fixtures.

If a tub or sink nearby gurgles or backs up as well, the problem sits in the branch or main drain, and the right tool is a cable machine or a camera. Our drain cleaning crews handle that kind of blockage, and a toilet that backs up repeatedly can point to a bigger line problem that main line cleaning addresses.

  • Never flush wipes marketed as flushable, paper towels, or feminine products.
  • Keep a toilet auger in the house and use it gently, pushing until you feel the clog give.

Fixing the toilet or replacing it

Repair makes sense when the porcelain is sound and the trouble sits in inexpensive internal parts. It makes less sense when the toilet has a cracked tank or bowl, a leak that comes through the porcelain itself, or repeated clogs from an old high-volume design. A cracked bowl is a replacement job, not a repair.

Replacement is also worth thinking about when parts are hard to find or the same toilet has needed several fixes. The plumber can install a new one during the same visit.

What shapes the price of a toilet repair

Several things move the final number. A simple flapper or fill valve swap is a small job. Pulling the toilet to replace a seal takes longer, and a damaged flange or rotted subfloor adds repair work beneath it. After-hours calls can differ from daytime visits.

On insurance: homeowners policies generally respond to sudden accidental damage, such as an overflow that ruins flooring. They usually do not cover wear and tear or the cost of fixing the failed part. Policies differ, so check yours.
