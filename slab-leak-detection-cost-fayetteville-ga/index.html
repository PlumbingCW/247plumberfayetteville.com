Slab Leak Detection Cost in Fayetteville, GA: What Drives It

Written by the Fayetteville 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 23, 2026.

The cost of slab leak detection in Fayetteville, GA comes down to a few factors. These are floor access, house size, the detection method, and whether you want the leak only found or also fixed.

We do not post dollar figures here because they would be guesses. What we can do is show you what moves a quote up or down, so you can read one with confidence.

A slab leak is a water line that has started leaking in or under the concrete floor of your home. Finding it is its own job, separate from repairing it. Below you will see what a fair quote lists, which signs justify paying for detection, and what to do while you wait for a plumber.

Locating a slab leak is not the same job as repairing it

Detection answers one question: where exactly is the water escaping? The plumber listens, scans, and tests until a spot on the floor plan is marked. That work stands alone.

Repair is a second job with its own scope. It might mean opening the floor above the leak, rerouting the line through the attic or walls, or replacing the whole run. A quote that blends the two without separate lines makes it hard to compare, so ask for them apart.

How the method changes the work

Leak detection uses acoustic listening devices, thermal cameras, pressure tests on the water lines, and sometimes tracer gas. A plumber rarely needs all of them. A pressure test can confirm that a line is losing water. A listening device then narrows down where the sound is loudest.

Thermal imaging helps when a hot water line is the culprit, since the warm path shows against cooler concrete. Tracer gas is slower but can pinpoint a leak when sound travels poorly. Stubborn leaks need more methods, and that is a fair reason for a higher quote.

Signs that justify paying for detection

Detection is worth the money when the signs point to hidden water under the house. These are the ones homeowners describe most:

  • Meter spinning: the water meter keeps moving after every tap and appliance is off.
  • Warm floor spot: a patch of floor that stays warm, often over a hot water line.
  • Creeping bill: the water bill climbs and no one is using more water.
  • Water sound: a hiss or trickle near the floor when the house is quiet.
  • Wet edges: damp baseboards, lifting flooring, or a musty smell with no visible source.
  • Low pressure: a drop in pressure that shows up at every fixture, not just one.

One sign alone can have a simple cause, like a running toilet. Two or three together are a good reason to book detection before the damage spreads.

What you can do before the plumber arrives

You can save time and limit damage with a few safe steps. Turn off every tap and appliance, then look at the meter. If it still moves, water is going somewhere. Shut the main valve if the floor is getting wet, and move rugs and furniture off the damp area.

Do not cut into the floor or drill to look for the pipe. You can damage a line or a conduit. Take clear photos of any wet spots, then leave the rest to the plumber.

If you smell gas at any point, leave the building and call 911 or the gas utility first. Call a plumber only after you are safe.

Honest limits and the insurance angle

Detection does not always end in a quick fix. Some leaks sit under a wall, a cabinet, or a bathtub. In older homes, the pipe itself may be worn out, and a spot repair would only move the problem down the line. A plumber should tell you when the honest answer is a reroute or a repipe, not another patch.

On insurance, homeowners policies generally respond to sudden accidental damage, not to wear and tear or the repair of the failed pipe itself. Policies differ, so check yours. A written detection report is often useful when you speak to your insurer.
