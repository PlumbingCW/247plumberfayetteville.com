Why a shopping center is its own plumbing job

A center splits plumbing between a landlord and many tenants. Your lease usually says who handles fixtures inside your unit and who handles shared lines. Read it before you call so the right party pays and nobody works on a line they do not own.

Shared drains matter too. A backup in one unit can show up in the next, so a main line problem is worth reporting to the landlord quickly.

Restaurants and food service

Kitchens put grease, food solids and hot water into the drains every hour. Grease traps and interceptors keep fats out of the sewer, and the rules for them are set by the local water utility and sewer authority. Ask them for the current requirements.

Floor drains and kitchen lines also clog from buildup. Hydro jetting and sewer line cleaning clear heavy buildup, and a camera look shows whether a line is damaged.

What to have ready before you call

  • Where it is: the unit number or the shared area, such as a back hallway or a service corridor.
  • What you see: water on the floor, a smell, a slow drain or no hot water, and when it started.
  • Whose line it is: inside your unit or shared with neighbors, if your lease says.
  • Access: who can let a plumber in and which hours the space is open.

Shops, offices and services

  • Restrooms: running toilets and leaking faucets waste water all day. See toilet repair.
  • Hot water: a failing heater affects restrooms and break rooms. See water heater repair.
  • Hidden leaks: an odd water bill can mean a leak behind a wall, and leak detection can find it.
  • Backflow devices: testing rules come from the water utility, so ask them what applies to your unit.
